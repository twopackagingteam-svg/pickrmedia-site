Why the Cheapest Test & Balance Bid Costs You More

A rock-bottom quote isn't a deal — it's a risk that gets quietly transferred onto your building. Here's what actually separates a bid priced to win from one priced to perform.

Every test and balance proposal has a price on it. None of them have an accuracy score. That's the problem — the one number you can compare instantly is the one that tells you the least about whether the work will actually be right.

Why Price Comparisons Mislead You Here

A test and balance report is a set of numbers on a page: airflow readings, water flow readings, adjustments made. Two firms can hand you a report that looks identical in format and wildly different in whether the numbers underneath it are true. The only way to find out which one you got is to live in the building afterward — hot and cold calls from tenants, a critical room that fails its next accreditation survey, equipment running harder than it should because the system was never actually balanced to spec. By the time the cheap bid reveals what it actually bought you, the firm that wrote it is long gone and the invoice has been paid.

That's why price, taken alone, isn't a comparison — it's a guess. The real comparison happens one level down, in the certification, the process, and what the firm is willing to stand behind.

Five Signs a Bid Was Priced to Win, Not to Perform

  • No NEBB or AABC registration number offered up front. A certified firm leads with it, because it's the one credential a low-cost operator can't produce.
  • A number that's dramatically below every other bid for the same scope. Balancing a building takes calibrated instruments and field time; a bid that undercuts the market by a wide margin is usually cutting one of those two, not finding efficiency.
  • No warranty on the work, or a vague "we'll come back if there's a problem." A firm confident in its own numbers will put a specific term on paper. Most Arizona TAB firms don't publish a warranty at all — that silence is informative.
  • A timeline that doesn't match the size of the system. Chasing a discrepancy to its actual source — a stuck damper, a mis-sized coil, a control sequence fighting itself — takes hours a rushed schedule doesn't have.
  • A generic, boilerplate report instead of instrument-by-instrument documented readings. If the paperwork could have been written before the technician ever showed up, the technician's visit didn't change much.

What Doing It Right Actually Costs — and Why It's Still Fair

Certified technicians, instruments that are actually recalibrated on schedule, and enough field time to chase every discrepancy to its real cause instead of settling for "close enough" all cost money. So does honest reporting — telling an owner or engineer about a design or installation problem the report reveals, even when that's an inconvenient thing to put in writing. None of that is padding. It's the difference between a report that reflects what's actually happening in the building and one that reflects what was easiest to write down.

Being fair on price and being the cheapest bid are not the same thing, and they're not supposed to be. A firm that backs its work with a real warranty has to be right the first time — a bargain operation racing to the bottom on price can't afford to make that promise, because it can't afford to honor it.
